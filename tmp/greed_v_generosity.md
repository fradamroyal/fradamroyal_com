# Greed vs. Generosity (Poverty of Spirit)

Greed enslaves the heart; generosity frees it. The Lord’s clear warning—“Take care to guard against all greed, for though one may be rich, one’s life does not consist of possessions” (Lk 12:15, NABRE)—names the spiritual battle at the core of discipleship. For Franciscans, that battle is not waged by contempt for the world, but by joyful dependence upon God and an open-handed love for neighbor.

## 1. Teaching on the Vice

The Catechism calls avarice (greed) one of the “capital” sins because it generates other sins (CCC 1866). It defines greed as the “desire to amass earthly goods without limit,” along with the craving for the power such goods bestow (CCC 2536). Greed is not merely a problem of the wealthy; it is a problem of the heart, which can cling as fiercely to a little as to a lot. Though our bank accounts vary, the heart’s attachments can be equally tight.

How does greed show up? Often subtly. It appears as the gnawing anxiety that we never have “enough,” the compulsion to upgrade what already works, the quiet habit of comparison that erodes gratitude, the refusal to be interrupted by a person in need because it would “cost” time. Greed narrows the gaze. It trains us to ask first, “What do I get?” and rarely, “What may I give?” Jesus diagnoses the root: “Where your treasure is, there also will your heart be” (Mt 6:21, NABRE). If our “treasure” is security, status, or control, the heart becomes captive to what moth and decay destroy (cf. Mt 6:19–21).

Greed also rewrites the story we tell ourselves about safety. We begin to believe that financial buffers—good in themselves—are ultimate. St. Paul warns that “the love of money is the root of all evils,” and that those who chase it “have strayed from the faith and have pierced themselves with many pains” (1 Tm 6:10, NABRE). Love of money, not money itself, corrodes the soul because it competes with love of God and neighbor.

Against this, the Gospel offers a different arithmetic. “Sell your belongings and give alms,” Jesus says. “Provide money bags for yourselves that do not wear out—an inexhaustible treasure in heaven” (Lk 12:33, NABRE). Generosity relocates our treasure and so reorients the heart (cf. Acts 20:35).

## 2. Franciscan Insight

Saint Francis learned evangelical freedom by letting go. When his father brought their dispute before the bishop, Francis returned even the clothes on his back and stood naked before him—an enacted homily that he would no longer be bound by claims of possession but would belong entirely to the Father in heaven. Thomas of Celano recounts the renunciation; the fuller account in *The Legend of the Three Companions* describes the prior legal dispute and Francis’s declaration that henceforth he would acknowledge God, rather than Pietro di Bernardone, as his Father.[^1]

The shape of Francis’s freedom was not merely private simplicity; it became a communal way. In the Later Rule he gave his brothers this crisp principle of “non-appropriation”: “Let the brothers not make anything their own, neither house, nor place, nor anything at all. As pilgrims and strangers in this world,” they were to serve the Lord in poverty and humility.[^2] This was not a rejection of creation but a refusal to be mastered by it. Francis grounds this “sublime height of most exalted poverty” in the poverty of Christ, who “made Himself poor in this world” (cf. 2 Cor 8:9).[^2]

He lived what he taught. In his Testament, Francis recalls that those who “came to receive life gave whatever they had to the poor” and were content with one tunic: “We desired nothing more.”[^3] Here we glimpse poverty not as dour deprivation but as interior spaciousness. “We desired nothing more”: a heart unburdened can love without calculating the cost.

The anonymous early Franciscan allegory *The Sacred Exchange between Saint Francis and Lady Poverty* expresses the same truth in a poetic, courtly-love key. Its narrator portrays Francis seeking the beloved: “Tell me, I beg you, where does Lady Poverty dwell? Where does she eat? Where does she rest at noon, for I languish with love of her?”[^4] Poverty—understood as evangelical detachment—becomes a bride because it binds the heart to its true Spouse, Christ. Bonaventure later writes that “holy poverty” made the brothers “prompt for every obedience, robust for work, and free for travel.” Because they possessed nothing earthly, they were held back by neither fear of loss nor distracting care.[^5]

For Secular Franciscans living in the world, Francis’s “non-appropriation” does not impose the friars’ juridical form of poverty. The Secular Franciscan Rule calls them to seek “a proper spirit of detachment from temporal goods” by simplifying their material needs and remembering that they are stewards of goods received for all.[^6] The Catechism similarly teaches that the “poverty of heart” required for entrance into the Kingdom asks Christ’s faithful to “direct their affections rightly,” lest use become attachment (CCC 2544–2545), and it celebrates the “joy of the poor,” to whom the Kingdom already belongs (CCC 2546–2547).

Francis’s Admonitions point in the same direction. One teaches humility: “For what a person is before God, that he is and no more.” Another addresses the present vice directly: “Where there is poverty with joy, there is neither greed nor avarice.”[^7] When God is enough, more no longer bargains for our worship.

## 3. The Opposing Virtue

The virtue opposed to greed is generosity, which in the Gospel is simply love put into circulation. Jesus blesses “the poor in spirit” (Mt 5:3)—the disciple whose heart is not owned by what he owns. The standard is Christ himself: “For your sake he became poor although he was rich, so that by his poverty you might become rich” (2 Cor 8:9, NABRE). The Church therefore teaches that love for the poor is part of her “constant tradition” (CCC 2444), that we owe the poor what is theirs as a matter of justice (CCC 2446), and that almsgiving is “a work of justice pleasing to God” (CCC 2447).

Here are five concrete Franciscan practices—simple enough for busy lay people—to cultivate this liberating poverty of spirit:
1. **First-fruits generosity.** Decide on a percentage of income to set aside first for alms and works of mercy—then build the budget around what remains. Do this as a family or fraternity so it becomes communal witness. Paul quotes Jesus: “It is more blessed to give than to receive” (Acts 20:35, NABRE).
2. **A joy-filled “simplicity audit.”** Choose one domain this month—closets, pantry, toolshed, subscriptions, or screens—and ask: What do we actually use? What can serve someone else better? Give good things away with gratitude, and let the space you create become room for prayer and people (cf. Lk 12:33).
3. **The daily examen of non-appropriation.** In night prayer, briefly review the day with Francis’s wisdom: What did I grasp? Where did I cling? Where did I receive as gift? Conclude with the Beatitude—“Blessed are the poor in spirit” (Mt 5:3)—asking for the grace to want less and love more tomorrow.
4. **Hospitality on the calendar.** Set a regular date—monthly or every six weeks—for simple hospitality aimed at someone who cannot repay: a newcomer, a widower, a family burdened by care (cf. Lk 14:12–14). The works of mercy are not seasonal; they are the Church’s ordinary language of love (CCC 2447).
5. **Carry an “alms plan.”** Keep small gift cards, bus passes, or a modest cash envelope designated for spontaneous giving. Pair alms with a look in the eyes and a prayer. This concrete readiness trains the heart to move from calculation to compassion, the very movement that poverty of spirit makes possible (cf. Lk 12:33; CCC 2547).

## Bringing It Together

Greed promises control and delivers anxiety; generosity risks loss and discovers freedom. The Beatitudes name this paradox, and Francis lived it with disarming literalness. He stripped himself to say with his body: the Father is enough. Radical non-appropriation did not make destitution an end in itself; it freed the brothers for God, fraternity, and mission.[^2][^5] The Testament’s serene line—“We desired nothing more”[^3]—is not a vow of deprivation; it is a confession of abundance.

For Secular Franciscans, the path is unmistakably in the world: family budgets, honest labor, and responsible stewardship remain.[^6] But the interior stance changes: what we have we hold for others; what we cannot share might be sharing us. The Catechism says our affections must be directed “rightly” (CCC 2545). Poverty of spirit is that right direction. It names a heart that treasures Christ and his poor above all, and so can rejoice when someone else receives what we could have kept.

“Where your treasure is, there also will your heart be” (Mt 6:21). The Franciscan question, then, is simple and searching: Where is my treasure today? If we consent to the Spirit’s work, generosity will steadily relocate it—toward the Kingdom that does not wear out, and toward the people for whom the King became poor (cf. Lk 12:33; 2 Cor 8:9).
