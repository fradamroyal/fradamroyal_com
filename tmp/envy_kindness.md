# Envy vs. Kindness: Building Franciscan Fraternity

*A Franciscan reflection on overcoming comparison with brotherly love*

Envy disrupts fraternity; kindness builds it. One of the enemy’s most corrosive tactics is envy, while one of the Spirit’s most beautiful fruits is kindness (Gal 5:22).[^1] In a distinctly Franciscan key, we could say: envy steals another’s song, while kindness amplifies it—so the whole fraternity can sing in harmony. Francis taught the brothers to recognize every good in another as the Lord’s own work.[^12] The task before us is to exchange the “evil eye” of comparison for the bright gaze of brotherly love.

## I. Naming the Vice: How Envy Appears

The Catechism names envy as a capital sin and defines it plainly as “sadness at the sight of another’s goods and the immoderate desire to acquire them for oneself, even unjustly.”[^2] In common modern usage, jealousy fears losing what it has, while envy grieves that another has something desirable. Envy does not simply want what another enjoys; it resents that the other should enjoy it. For that reason, envy is anti-fraternal by nature: it makes a brother or sister into a rival and quietly wishes that the other be diminished so that the self might feel larger.

Scripture gives sober portraits of envy’s logic. Cain cannot bear Abel’s favor (Gen 4:4–8). Saul’s heart turns against David as the women sing of David’s victories (1 Sam 18:7–9). In the vineyard, the landowner asks the murmurers, “Do you begrudge my generosity?”[^3] St. Paul teaches that “love is patient and kind; love is not jealous or boastful,”[^4] and warns, “Let us have no self-conceit, no provoking of one another, no envy of one another” (Gal 5:26). St. James is blunt: “For where jealousy and selfish ambition exist, there will be disorder and every vile practice.”[^5] The tradition even traces the wound of death itself to envy: “through the devil’s envy death entered the world.”[^6]

In ordinary life, envy rarely shows itself with horns. It tends to wear the pleasant mask of comparison. It can sound like a subtle discounting of someone’s success—“Yes, but you should have seen the mistakes”—or like indifference to a brother’s gift—“That’s just his thing.” It can hide in a tone of perpetual critique, or in a low-grade gloom whenever others receive attention. It can lurk in spiritual circles too: we may begrudge the charisms of another, or quietly wish that our project, our community, our ministry were the one everyone noticed. In the digital age, curated images of other people’s lives can intensify the habit of measuring, comparing, and aching.

The Catechism proposes good will and humility. Envy “represents a form of sadness and therefore a refusal of charity,” it teaches; the baptized struggle against it by “exercising good will” and learning to live in humility.[^7] In other words, the antidote to envy is not denial of the other’s gifts but gratitude for them, offered to God who is the giver of every good. Only such gratitude frees us from the fearful economy of comparison and returns us to the abundance of the Gospel: “All things are yours; you are Christ’s; and Christ is God’s.”[^8]

## II. Franciscan Insight: Minority and Joy in Another’s Gifts

Francis called his companions *fratres minores*, “lesser brothers,” a name that breathes a whole spirituality. In the Earlier Rule he directed, “Let no one be called ‘prior,’ but let everyone in general be called a lesser brother. Let one wash the feet of the other.” Thomas of Celano reports that this Rule text supplied the fraternity’s name.[^9] Minority is the evangelical decision to take the lower place and serve. It is the posture of one who is more interested in another’s flourishing than in one’s own prominence. That is why minority is a deep remedy for envy; the lesser brother does not measure his worth by comparison but by conformity to Christ, “who came not to be served but to serve.”[^10]

From the beginning, Francis perceived that the Lord was fashioning a fraternity. In his Testament he remembers that “the Lord gave me some brothers,”[^11] a phrase that acknowledges both gift and otherness. Because they are given, the brothers are not mine to control or resent. Because they are brothers, their gifts are not threats but provisions for the common table. Francis’s Admonition VIII names envy with startling precision: whoever envies the good that the Lord says or does in a brother thereby envies the Most High. A later source, *A Mirror of Perfection*, portrays Francis honoring the distinctive virtues of Bernard, Leo, Angelo, Masseo, Giles, Rufino, Juniper, and other brothers.[^12] Franciscan fraternity rejoices when God’s grace takes a form different from one’s own.

A beloved scene from the *Little Flowers* captures Francis’s freedom from comparison: Brother Masseo, perplexed at Francis’s popularity, asks why people flock to him. Francis replies that the Most High chose him precisely to confound nobility and wisdom—a debtor to mercy who cannot boast.[^13] The one who knows he has received everything as alms does not begrudge another’s portion. Gratitude makes the heart large. In that largeness, others’ gifts are recognized as God’s artistry, not as competitors’ trophies.

St. Clare embodies a similar logic. Her letters to Agnes of Prague are a sustained act of encouragement. In the Third Letter Clare says she is “filled with such great joy” at Agnes’s well-being and progress, adding that no one can separate her from this joy. In the Second Letter she urges Agnes: “What you hold, may you continue to hold, what you do, may you keep doing and not stop.”[^14] She does not clutch her own calling as a private possession but rejoices that the Lord multiplies it in another. That is Franciscan kindness.

Francis’s *Canticle of the Creatures* also tutors the heart out of envy. By teaching us to praise God “with all Your creatures, especially Sir Brother Sun” and “through Sister Water,”[^15] he trains us to see the world not as a race of competitors but as a choir of gifts. In a choir, one voice’s strength enriches the whole; the alto does not resent the soprano’s high notes. To live as “lesser” is to love the whole choir’s sound more than one’s own solo. The grace given to you is for me too, because it builds up the one Body (cf. 1 Cor 12:4–7, 12–27).

In 1224 on La Verna, Francis received the stigmata, sealing his conformity to the humble and crucified Christ.[^16] The marks of love do not license superiority; they call us more deeply into the self-giving love of Christ. We ask for the same grace: to rejoice in each other’s graces.

## III. The Opposing Virtue: Cultivating Kindness in Daily Practice

In Galatians 5:22 St. Paul names *chrēstotēs*, “kindness”; in 1 Corinthians 13:4 he uses the cognate verb: love “is kind.” Kindness is not sentimentality; it is a stable habit of willing another’s flourishing and acting to further it. In a Franciscan key, kindness is the concrete shape of fraternity: brotherly love made ordinary. The following practices cultivate it.

1. **Name the comparison and bless the gift.** When the “evil eye” stirs—at a colleague’s success, a parish’s growth, a brother’s talents—pause and explicitly bless the good you see. Say aloud, “Blessed be God for this grace in this person.” This concretizes the good will commended by the Catechism.[^7] The tongue that blesses retrains the heart to rejoice.
2. **Practice the examen of gratitude for others.** Each evening, thank God for three graces you noticed in other people that day: a sister’s patience, a volunteer’s craft, a child’s trust. Then ask to see more tomorrow. This simple discipline loosens envy’s grip and opens a generous vision of the Body.
3. **“Outdo one another in showing honor.”**[^17] Make it a habit to highlight someone else’s contribution before your own. In meetings, name what another did well; in presentations or community gatherings, give thanks publicly for shared work. Such honor-forward speech builds a culture where gifts are communal.
4. **Fast from murmuring.** Francis’s Later Rule explicitly warns the brothers against “detraction and murmuring,” as well as envy.[^18] Choose a fast: no detraction, no sarcasm about a brother, no rolling of eyes. When complaint rises, convert it into intercession: “Lord, increase the good you have begun in him.” This is the kindness of the tongue.
5. **Choose minority in concrete ways.** Take the lower place at table. Volunteer for hidden tasks. Let another take the lead on a project. Wash dishes without remark; write a note of thanks and sign no name. Minority undercuts the economy of comparison and frees us for joy in others.
6. **Celebrate others’ feasts.** Mark the anniversaries and milestones of those around you. Bake the cake, write the card, organize the blessing. Kindness becomes culture through shared celebration, and celebration inoculates against envy by turning gifts into common joy.
7. **Mentor and make room.** If you have a platform—at work, in your fraternity, parish, family, or neighborhood—lend it. Invite younger voices to share their story, facilitate, organize, or lead a project. Ask how you can help them thrive as Secular Franciscans. The Franciscan tradition loves the multiplication of gifts; kindness sees potential and fosters it, trusting that the Spirit is prodigal.
8. **Receive your life as alms.** Return often to the Apostle’s question: “What have you that you did not receive?”[^19] Poverty of spirit relativizes comparison. When my own talents are received rather than achieved, I am free to admire another’s without anxiety. Begin the day with the prayer, “Everything is gift; make me generous.”
9. **Pray against the spirit of envy.** Ask the Lord to uproot the lie of scarcity and to give you joy in others’ good. Name the relationships where comparison haunts you and place them under his lordship. Spiritual combat is real; so is grace.
10. **Contemplate Christ’s kindness.** Gaze, with Clare, upon the humility of God in the manger and on the cross. Christ “emptied himself, taking the form of a servant” (Phil 2:7). Let his eyes become yours. In adoration, ask to see as he sees: every person as a beloved gift for the whole.

St. Paul’s hymn to charity provides a sure measure: “Love is patient and kind; love is not jealous or boastful; it is not arrogant or rude.”[^4] Kindness and envy cannot share the same house. To build a Franciscan house, then, is to practice the many small courtesies of love—the cheerful greeting, the quick apology, the readiness to forgive, the willingness to yield a preference, the habit of noticing gifts. Such courtesies are not trivial; they are the carpentry of fraternity. In such a house, kindness is not a mood but a way of life, and envy has no place to stand. Love, finally, “does not rejoice at wrong, but rejoices in the right.”[^20]
