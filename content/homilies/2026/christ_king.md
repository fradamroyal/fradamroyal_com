Both groups, the sheep and the goats, live in a kind of blindness. They do not recognize that Christ is before them. When the Lord tells them what they have done, both groups respond, “But when did we see you?” Their question tells us that what truly matters in this moment is their hearts.

The sheep, those who are going off to eternal life, have been transformed. The same fire of love that burns within the heart of Christ now burns within theirs. They saw the hungry, the naked, the stranger, and they acted. They expected no reward in the future, and certainly none in that moment. They acted simply because they love. The grace of God has remade them in the image of Christ. And so their very habits, their actions, their choices all reflect what Christ himself would do.

The goats did not see Christ before them either. They saw no possible reward for doing good, so they acted selfishly. They refused to help. They refused to be transformed and remained in their fallen human nature. What we see in them is pure selfishness. They show neither the love of God nor even human love. And so they go off to the second death.

Christianity is about transformation. It is about the total re-creation of the human person in the image of God, that is, in the image of Christ. And sometimes we lose sight of that. We get caught up in the idea that Christianity is just a moral system, that it is about how to be a good person, about what you should and should not do. Anyone can be good. We are called to be perfect. We are called to be like God himself, and that is something God alone can give us.

Even if someone could live his whole life avoiding sin and never be a slave to it, he would still need God to give him charity and make him a new creation in Christ. We are called to more than avoiding sin and living by a moral framework. We are called to be like God. It is essential that we understand this, because it changes our whole relationship to the world.

We fight against sin in our own lives for a deeper reason than the rule against it. We fight because sin stands in the way of our goal, which is transformation. Sin darkens the intellect. It makes it harder to recognize the good and holy path, even when it is right in front of us. It interferes with the cultivation of charity. And so we push against it to make room for charity, for the love of God. The mere eradication of sin falls short of our goal for ourselves and for the world.

Today is the feast of Christ the King. We recognize Christ as King of the whole universe, King of all creation, King of everything that exists. His kingdom is unlike any earthly kingdom. It does not rest on laws and rules. It is about a relationship. It is about drawing closer to him and closer to one another. It is about imitating his life and becoming like him.

It would not matter how many laws we passed in this world. It would not matter if we could pull this entire culture from death toward life. It would not matter if every single sin were illegal and punished to the highest possible degree. That would not make this world Christian. Only the grace of God can do that. Christ’s kingdom comes about when we welcome that grace into our hearts and let it transform us so that we love as he does.

That is what we see in this Gospel scene, and that is what will happen at the end of this age. Christ will separate those who truly sought his kingdom by being remade in his image from those who did anything else.

We are called to follow Christ our King. The sword cannot achieve that. Laws and political power cannot achieve it. It will happen within our own hearts, by truly becoming like him, by becoming servants as he was, by going out and actively living his life in this world. That is the only thing that will bring the change that we say we hope for. It is the only thing that will draw us closer to eternal life.
