When we hear the word *sloth*, we may think simply of laziness. But Scripture and the Christian tradition speak of something deeper. They speak of *acedia*: a heaviness of soul that resists the demands of love.

## When Love Grows Heavy

The Catechism describes spiritual sloth as refusing the joy that comes from God and being repelled by divine goodness.{{< sidenote >}} CCC 2094. Catechism citations refer to the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*, 2nd ed. {{< /sidenote >}} In prayer, acedia can arise from lax ascetical practice, decreased vigilance, and carelessness of heart.{{< sidenote >}} CCC 2733. {{< /sidenote >}} It may appear as procrastination, distraction, or chronic inconstancy: a recurring paralysis before the “next faithful step.”

We have to distinguish this clearly from legitimate rest, bodily illness, or clinical depression. Those require compassionate discernment and care, not blame. Acedia is something different: the temptation to turn away from the good that love asks of us.

The wisdom books show us its ordinary face. “Go to the ant, O sluggard, study her ways and learn wisdom.”{{< sidenote >}} Prov 6:6–11. Scripture quotations are from the New American Bible, Revised Edition (NABRE). {{< /sidenote >}} Sloth is not merely inactivity. It is a habit of postponement that drains vitality and erodes hope. It promises rest but delivers restlessness. The vineyard of the sluggard lies neglected until “poverty will come upon you like a robber.”{{< sidenote >}} Prov 24:30–34. {{< /sidenote >}}

The New Testament sharpens the point: “Do not grow slack in zeal, be fervent in spirit, serve the Lord.”{{< sidenote >}} Rom 12:11. {{< /sidenote >}} There is a real battle here. Our zeal fades when we lose sight of the Lord’s nearness and the dignity of the work he has placed in our hands.

Acedia can even hide within frantic busyness. Someone can be “always on,” constantly occupied, and still avoid what Jesus calls the one needful thing: “There is need of only one thing.”{{< sidenote >}} Lk 10:42. {{< /sidenote >}} If nothing is offered to God, all that activity can leave us spiritually motionless. Sloth is not only a failure to act; it is a failure to love. In the Lord’s parable, the servant who buried his talent did not fall into some spectacular vice. He simply refused the risk of fruitful love.{{< sidenote >}} Mt 25:14–30. {{< /sidenote >}}

Jesus gives us the opposite posture when he says, “Gird your loins and light your lamps.”{{< sidenote >}} Lk 12:35. {{< /sidenote >}} We are to be ready, alert, and responsive to God’s call in each moment.

At times, sloth grows out of discouragement. We may begin prayer or ministry with vigor, only to have setbacks cool our hearts. The Letter to the Hebrews urges Christians not to “become sluggish,” but to imitate those who through faith and patience inherit the promises.{{< sidenote >}} Heb 6:11–12. {{< /sidenote >}} The remedy is not gritted teeth. It is to contemplate God’s fidelity again and allow hope to be renewed.

When charity is renewed, diligence follows: “Whatever you do, do from the heart, as for the Lord.”{{< sidenote >}} Col 3:23. {{< /sidenote >}} The Catechism likewise teaches that human work participates in the Creator’s work and becomes a path of sanctification when joined to Christ.{{< sidenote >}} CCC 2427. {{< /sidenote >}} Diligence is not simply productivity. It is the steady response of love to God’s love.

Sloth also corrodes community. It tempts us to withdraw from shared responsibilities and let someone else carry the load. St. Paul answers, “Let us not grow tired of doing good.”{{< sidenote >}} Gal 6:9. {{< /sidenote >}} In our families, fraternities, workplaces, and parishes, charity becomes visible through steady faithfulness: showing up on time, praying with attention, keeping a promise, or doing the humble task no one sees. The action may be small, but Christ makes it an act of love.

## Franciscan Zeal Begins Again

St. Francis saw the Gospel not as an ideal to admire but as a life to be lived. Thomas of Celano says that, after Francis heard the Gospel instructions for the disciples’ mission, he “did not suffer any delay” before putting them into practice. Near the end of his life, Francis still said, “Let us begin, brothers, to serve the Lord God, for up until now we have done little or nothing.”{{< sidenote >}} Thomas of Celano, *The Life of Saint Francis* 22, 103, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, *The Saint*, ed. Regis J. Armstrong, J. A. Wayne Hellmann, and William J. Short (Hyde Park, NY: New City Press, 1999), 201–202, 273; cf. Bonaventure, *Major Legend* XIV.1, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 2, *The Founder*, ed. Regis J. Armstrong, J. A. Wayne Hellmann, and William J. Short (Hyde Park, NY: New City Press, 2000), 640. {{< /sidenote >}} These words do not express restlessness. They express evangelical freshness. With the grace of God, every day can become a new beginning in conversion and mission.

Francis’s zeal was never frantic activity. He guarded the primacy of prayer and the interior life. In the Later Rule, he charges the brothers who have the grace of labor to “work faithfully and devotedly” in such a way that they “do not extinguish the spirit of holy prayer and devotion.”{{< sidenote >}} Francis of Assisi, *Later Rule* V.1–4, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 102–103. {{< /sidenote >}} Work must not smother prayer, and prayer must not become a pretext for evading the work of love. When they are properly ordered, prayer and work belong together. Diligent service becomes the fruit of contemplation.

Bonaventure describes Francis as never idle in doing good, dividing his time between service of his neighbors and contemplation. Near death, unable to walk, Francis had himself carried through towns to encourage others and longed to serve lepers again.{{< sidenote >}} Bonaventure, *Major Legend* XIII.1; XIV.1, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 2, 630–631, 640. {{< /sidenote >}} This does not make illness culpable or deny the body’s need for care. It shows us a love that continues to seek the good within real limitations.

Celano gives us an even more ordinary example. During winter at Celano, Francis immediately gave a poor old woman the borrowed cloth he wore and asked his companion to add his own so that she could complete a tunic.{{< sidenote >}} Thomas of Celano, *The Remembrance of the Desire of a Soul* 86, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 2. {{< /sidenote >}} For Francis, diligence meant responding to the concrete neighbor who stood before him.

That same zeal marked the early movement. Francis sent brothers two by two to preach penance and peace, entrusting the outcome to the Lord.{{< sidenote >}} Thomas of Celano, *The Life of Saint Francis* 29–30, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1. {{< /sidenote >}} Yet their readiness remained joined to discernment and humility. The Later Rule directs the brothers to work with their hands and permits them to receive what is necessary for bodily support, except coin or money; when necessary, they may go for alms.{{< sidenote >}} Francis of Assisi, *Later Rule* V.1–4, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 102–103. {{< /sidenote >}} Work and begging are distinct in the Rule, but both are ordered to dependence on God and freedom from possessiveness.

St. Clare echoes this same spirit. She urges Agnes to go forward “with swift pace, nimble step, and feet that do not stumble,” tranquilly, joyfully, briskly, and cautiously along the path of happiness.{{< sidenote >}} Clare of Assisi, *Second Letter to Agnes of Prague* 11–14, in *The Lady: Clare of Assisi: Early Documents*, 3rd ed., ed. and trans. Regis J. Armstrong (Hyde Park, NY: New City Press, 2006), 47. {{< /sidenote >}} For Clare, swiftness meant the prompt obedience of love: cheerful and steady rather than impulsive. She and Francis both expose our tendency to delay today’s small good while imagining tomorrow’s grand project. The Franciscan answer is concrete charity now.

We see the same practical diligence in Francis’s Eucharistic devotion. His *Exhortations to the Clergy* confronts the use of unsuitable vessels, dirty linens, and irreverent reservation of the Eucharist; his *First Letter to the Custodians* calls for precious vessels and linens and for the Lord’s Body to be properly reserved in a precious place.{{< sidenote >}} Francis of Assisi, *Exhortations to the Clergy* 1–5 and *First Letter to the Custodians* 2–4, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 52–57. {{< /sidenote >}} Francis would not let love remain a sentiment when reverence demanded action.

Finally, Franciscan diligence is inseparable from courtesy and peace. In his Testament, Francis recalls that the Lord revealed the greeting “May the Lord give you peace.” In the Later Rule, he instructs brothers going through the world not to quarrel or judge others, but to be “meek, peaceful, modest, gentle, and humble.”{{< sidenote >}} Francis of Assisi, *Testament* 23, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 125; *Later Rule* III.10–11, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 102. {{< /sidenote >}} Zeal that tramples people is not evangelical. Franciscan zeal builds communion through gratitude, peace, and one obedient act at a time.

## Diligence Is Steady Love

The virtue opposed to sloth is diligence: steadfast, prompt, joyful devotion to the duties of love. Diligence flows from charity and hope. Because we trust that God is at work, we can give ourselves to the work before us. It is not mere speed but constancy, and not anxiety but availability to grace.

“Be firm, steadfast, always fully devoted to the work of the Lord,” says St. Paul, “knowing that in the Lord your labor is not in vain.”{{< sidenote >}} 1 Cor 15:58. {{< /sidenote >}} Time is a gift entrusted to us, and daily work can become a place of sanctification when joined to Christ.{{< sidenote >}} Ps 90:12; CCC 2427. {{< /sidenote >}}

How, then, can a Secular Franciscan cultivate diligence in ordinary life? These five practices are simple, but they train the heart to answer love promptly.

1. **Consecrate the first tasks of the day to the Lord.** Begin by offering the day—its people, duties, and interruptions—to God. A brief morning prayer, perhaps prayed with the day’s Gospel, aligns desire and action: “Speak, Lord, for your servant is listening.”{{< sidenote >}} 1 Sam 3:9. {{< /sidenote >}} Then identify the “next faithful step” in each vocation: family, work, fraternity, and parish. Write those steps down. The purpose is not efficiency for its own sake, but stewardship of grace. Ask the Holy Spirit to help you do the first things first.{{< sidenote >}} Rom 12:11. {{< /sidenote >}} By presenting the day to God, we cut off acedia’s first whisper: “You can drift.”

2. **Keep prayer and work braided together.** Francis was adamant that labor should not extinguish prayer, and prayer should not evade labor.{{< sidenote >}} Francis of Assisi, *Later Rule* V.1–4, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 102–103. {{< /sidenote >}} Establish a small rhythm: the Angelus at midday, a decade of the rosary on a commute, or a Psalm before a difficult meeting. Offer frustrations as intercession and successes as thanksgiving. This protects us from both frenzy and avoidance, and it transforms hidden duties—emails, dishes, spreadsheets—into acts of love.{{< sidenote >}} Col 3:23. {{< /sidenote >}} When you notice yourself repeatedly delaying a particular task, pause and ask: *What fear or sadness lies beneath this?* Then invite Christ into that place. Diligence is born from grace received, not from self-accusation.

3. **Practice prompt charity in one concrete way each day.** Choose a daily act that meets you at the edge of reluctance: return the difficult phone call, visit the person you are tempted to avoid, or give alms when you would prefer to pass by. Decide beforehand that, when such an opportunity appears, you will act within twenty-four hours—sooner when possible. Keep the act small and specific. This trained swiftness develops the watchfulness Jesus commends.{{< sidenote >}} Lk 12:35. {{< /sidenote >}} Over time, it counters the burial of the “talent”{{< sidenote >}} Mt 25:14–30. {{< /sidenote >}} and opens us to larger generosity.

4. **Embrace a Franciscan simplicity that lightens the will.** Material and digital clutter can scatter our attention and make even small duties feel heavy. Choose one space each week to simplify: your desk, your inbox, or your evening schedule. Let go of what does not serve your vocation. The point is freedom for love. Bonaventure says that holy poverty made the early brothers ready for obedience and work and free for travel.{{< sidenote >}} Bonaventure, *Major Legend* IV.7, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 2, 554. {{< /sidenote >}} Review your media habits regularly and set boundaries that protect both prayer and service.

5. **Close the day with gratitude and a small, honest examen.** Each evening, thank the Lord for two or three graces received. Then ask: *Where did I delay love? Where did I respond promptly?* Receive mercy where you failed, make one specific resolution for tomorrow, and go to rest with a simple act of trust.{{< sidenote >}} Ps 4:9. {{< /sidenote >}} This gentle accountability keeps zeal honest without feeding scrupulosity. Diligence is joyful perseverance, not grim perfectionism. Remember St. Paul’s promise: “The one who began a good work in you will continue to complete it until the day of Christ Jesus.”{{< sidenote >}} Phil 1:6. {{< /sidenote >}} Christ has begun the work; our part is to keep responding to his grace.

## Begin Again in Christ

Brothers and sisters, the path from sloth to diligence is not a sprint but a steady pilgrimage. We do not overcome acedia by self-reliance. Christ converts us by teaching us to receive and return his love. In the Eucharist he says, *hoc est corpus meum*—“this is my body.”{{< sidenote >}} Mt 26:26. {{< /sidenote >}} He gives himself totally; Francis therefore urges us to hold back nothing of ourselves from him.{{< sidenote >}} Francis of Assisi, *Letter to the Entire Order* 29, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 118. {{< /sidenote >}}

In that light, small acts—answering an email with patience, washing a dish with gratitude, listening without defensiveness—become acts of the Kingdom. In fraternity, we should encourage one another rather than pressuring one another, helping each other to “stir into flame the gift of God.”{{< sidenote >}} 2 Tim 1:6. {{< /sidenote >}}

When we stumble, we begin again—not because yesterday was worthless, but because the Lord’s mercies are renewed each morning.{{< sidenote >}} Lam 3:22–23. {{< /sidenote >}} Our families, parishes, and fraternities need that steady faithfulness, Franciscan courtesy, and ordinary, joyful diligence. “The Lord is near.”{{< sidenote >}} Phil 4:5. {{< /sidenote >}}
