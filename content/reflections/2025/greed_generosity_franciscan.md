The Lord warns us plainly: “Take care to guard against all greed, for though one may be rich, one’s life does not consist of possessions.”{{< sidenote >}} Lk 12:15, NABRE. Scripture quotations are from the New American Bible, Revised Edition (NABRE). {{< /sidenote >}} This is a spiritual battle for every disciple, not only for the wealthy. For Franciscans, the answer is not contempt for the world. It is joyful dependence on God and an open-handed love for our neighbor.

## When Possessions Possess Us

The Catechism calls avarice (greed) one of the “capital” sins because it generates other sins.{{< sidenote >}} CCC 1866. Catechism citations refer to the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*, 2nd ed. {{< /sidenote >}} It defines greed as the “desire to amass earthly goods without limit,” together with the desire for the power those goods can provide.{{< sidenote >}} CCC 2536. {{< /sidenote >}} Greed is a problem of the heart. A person can cling as tightly to a little as to a lot.

Most often, greed enters ordinary life quietly. It is the anxiety that says we never have “enough,” the urge to replace what still works, the habit of comparing our home or family to someone else’s, or the refusal to help because it will cost us time. It narrows our attention until our first question becomes, “What do I get?” rather than, “What may I give?”

Jesus points to the heart of the matter: “Where your treasure is, there also will your heart be.”{{< sidenote >}} Mt 6:21, NABRE. {{< /sidenote >}} If our “treasure” is security, status, or control, then our hearts become captive to what moth and decay destroy.{{< sidenote >}} cf. Mt 6:19–21. {{< /sidenote >}}

Greed also changes the story we tell ourselves about safety. Savings and prudent planning are good, but they are not our Savior. St. Paul warns that “the love of money is the root of all evils,” and that those who chase it “have strayed from the faith and have pierced themselves with many pains.”{{< sidenote >}} 1 Tm 6:10, NABRE. {{< /sidenote >}} Money itself is not the problem. The love of money corrodes the soul because it competes with love of God and neighbor.

Christ shows us another way. “Sell your belongings and give alms,” Jesus says. “Provide money bags for yourselves that do not wear out—an inexhaustible treasure in heaven.”{{< sidenote >}} Lk 12:33, NABRE. {{< /sidenote >}} When his grace makes us generous, our treasure moves, and the heart follows.{{< sidenote >}} cf. Acts 20:35. {{< /sidenote >}}

## The Freedom St. Francis Found

Saint Francis learned evangelical freedom by letting go. When his father brought their dispute before the bishop, Francis returned even the clothes on his back and stood naked before him. He would no longer be bound by claims of possession but would belong entirely to the Father in heaven. Thomas of Celano recounts the renunciation, while the fuller account in *The Legend of the Three Companions* describes the earlier legal dispute and Francis’s declaration that he would henceforth acknowledge God, rather than Pietro di Bernardone, as his Father.{{< sidenote >}} Thomas of Celano, *The Life of Saint Francis* I.6.14–15, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, *The Saint*, ed. Regis J. Armstrong, J. A. Wayne Hellmann, and William J. Short (Hyde Park, NY: New City Press, 1999), 193–194; *The Legend of the Three Companions* 19–20, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 2, *The Founder*, ed. Regis J. Armstrong, J. A. Wayne Hellmann, and William J. Short (Hyde Park, NY: New City Press, 2000), 79–80. {{< /sidenote >}} His renunciation was not an empty gesture. It made visible the freedom he had received from God.

That freedom did not remain a matter of private simplicity. It became a communal way of life. In the Later Rule, Francis gave his brothers a clear principle of “non-appropriation”: “Let the brothers not make anything their own, neither house, nor place, nor anything at all. As pilgrims and strangers in this world,” they were to serve the Lord in poverty and humility.{{< sidenote >}} Francis of Assisi, *Later Rule* VI.1–6, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 103. {{< /sidenote >}} They did not reject creation. They refused to let created things master them.

Francis rooted this “sublime height of most exalted poverty” in Christ, who “made Himself poor in this world.”{{< sidenote >}} cf. 2 Cor 8:9. Francis of Assisi, *Later Rule* VI.1–6, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 103. {{< /sidenote >}} Franciscan poverty begins with what Christ has done. The Son of God gives himself to us, and by grace we learn to loosen our grip.

Francis lived what he taught. In his Testament, he recalled that those who “came to receive life gave whatever they had to the poor” and were content with one tunic: “We desired nothing more.”{{< sidenote >}} Francis of Assisi, *Testament* 16–17, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 125. {{< /sidenote >}} That line does not describe dour deprivation. It describes a heart no longer burdened by constant wanting, a heart free to love without calculating the cost.

The anonymous early Franciscan allegory *The Sacred Exchange between Saint Francis and Lady Poverty* expresses the same truth in the language of courtly love. Its narrator portrays Francis seeking his beloved: “Tell me, I beg you, where does Lady Poverty dwell? Where does she eat? Where does she rest at noon, for I languish with love of her?”{{< sidenote >}} Anonymous, *The Sacred Exchange between Saint Francis and Lady Poverty* 9, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 531. {{< /sidenote >}} Poverty, understood as evangelical detachment, becomes a bride because it binds the heart to its true Spouse, Christ.

Bonaventure later wrote that “holy poverty” made the brothers “prompt for every obedience, robust for work, and free for travel.” Because they possessed nothing earthly, they were held back by neither fear of loss nor distracting care.{{< sidenote >}} Bonaventure, *Major Legend* IV.7, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 2, 554. {{< /sidenote >}} Their poverty left them ready for God, for work, and for mission.

Secular Franciscans live this freedom in a different form. Francis’s “non-appropriation” does not impose the friars’ juridical poverty on those who live in families, work at ordinary jobs, and care for homes. The Secular Franciscan Rule instead calls them to seek “a proper spirit of detachment from temporal goods” by simplifying their material needs and remembering that they are stewards of goods received for all.{{< sidenote >}} *Rule of the Secular Franciscan Order*, art. 11. {{< /sidenote >}} The Catechism likewise teaches that the “poverty of heart” required for entrance into the Kingdom asks Christ’s faithful to “direct their affections rightly,” lest use become attachment.{{< sidenote >}} CCC 2544–2545. {{< /sidenote >}} It also celebrates the “joy of the poor,” to whom the Kingdom already belongs.{{< sidenote >}} CCC 2546–2547. {{< /sidenote >}}

Francis’s Admonitions lead us in the same direction. One teaches humility: “For what a person is before God, that he is and no more.” Another speaks directly to this vice: “Where there is poverty with joy, there is neither greed nor avarice.”{{< sidenote >}} Francis of Assisi, *Admonition* XIX.2 and *Admonition* XXVII.3, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 135, 137. {{< /sidenote >}} When God is enough, the desire for more no longer commands our worship.

## Generosity in Daily Life

The virtue opposed to greed is generosity: love made concrete in what we give and how we receive. Jesus blesses “the poor in spirit,”{{< sidenote >}} Mt 5:3. {{< /sidenote >}} those whose hearts are not owned by what they own. Christ himself is our standard: “For your sake he became poor although he was rich, so that by his poverty you might become rich.”{{< sidenote >}} 2 Cor 8:9, NABRE. {{< /sidenote >}}

Because Christ has first given himself to us, generosity is more than private kindness. The Church teaches that love for the poor belongs to her “constant tradition,”{{< sidenote >}} CCC 2444. {{< /sidenote >}} that we owe the poor what is theirs as a matter of justice,{{< sidenote >}} CCC 2446. {{< /sidenote >}} and that almsgiving is “a work of justice pleasing to God.”{{< sidenote >}} CCC 2447. {{< /sidenote >}}

Here are five practices that can help poverty of spirit take root in family, parish, work, and fraternity life:

1. **Give first.** Decide on a percentage of income to set aside first for alms and works of mercy, then build the household budget around what remains. A family or fraternity can make this decision together so that generosity becomes a shared witness. Paul quotes Jesus: “It is more blessed to give than to receive.”{{< sidenote >}} Acts 20:35, NABRE. {{< /sidenote >}}
2. **Simplify one part of daily life.** Choose one area this month—a closet, pantry, toolshed, list of subscriptions, or use of screens. Ask what you actually use and what might serve someone else better. Give good things away with gratitude, and let the space you create become room for prayer and people.{{< sidenote >}} cf. Lk 12:33. {{< /sidenote >}}
3. **Examine attachment each night.** During night prayer, ask: What did I grasp? Where did I cling? Where did I receive as gift? End with the Beatitude—“Blessed are the poor in spirit”—and ask Christ for the grace to want less and love more tomorrow.{{< sidenote >}} Mt 5:3. {{< /sidenote >}}
4. **Make room for hospitality.** Choose a regular date—monthly or every six weeks—and welcome someone who cannot repay you: a newcomer, a widower, or a family burdened by care.{{< sidenote >}} cf. Lk 14:12–14. {{< /sidenote >}} The works of mercy are not seasonal. They are the Church’s ordinary language of love.{{< sidenote >}} CCC 2447. {{< /sidenote >}}
5. **Be ready to give.** Keep small gift cards, bus passes, or a modest cash envelope available for spontaneous giving. Offer the gift with a look in the eyes and a prayer. This readiness trains the heart to move from calculation to compassion, the movement that poverty of spirit makes possible.{{< sidenote >}} cf. Lk 12:33; CCC 2547. {{< /sidenote >}}

## Held by Christ

Greed promises control but leaves us anxious. Generosity may feel like a loss, but in Christ it becomes freedom. The Beatitudes name this paradox, and Francis lived it with striking simplicity. He stripped himself to say with his body that the Father was enough.

Radical non-appropriation{{< sidenote >}} Francis of Assisi, *Later Rule* VI.1–6, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 103. {{< /sidenote >}} did not make destitution an end in itself. It freed the brothers for God, fraternity, and mission.{{< sidenote >}} Bonaventure, *Major Legend* IV.7, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 2, 554. {{< /sidenote >}}

The Testament repeats the serene line “We desired nothing more”{{< sidenote >}} Francis of Assisi, *Testament* 16–17, in *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*, vol. 1, 125. {{< /sidenote >}} and it is not a vow of deprivation. It is the confession of people who have found their abundance in God.

For Secular Franciscans, this path remains firmly in the world. Family budgets, honest labor, and responsible stewardship still matter.{{< sidenote >}} *Rule of the Secular Franciscan Order*, art. 11. {{< /sidenote >}} What changes is the heart. We hold what we have for the good of others. When we cannot share something, it may have begun to possess us. The Catechism says that our affections must be directed “rightly.”{{< sidenote >}} CCC 2545. {{< /sidenote >}} Poverty of spirit is that right direction: a heart that treasures Christ and his poor above all and can rejoice when someone else receives what we could have kept.

“Where your treasure is, there also will your heart be.”{{< sidenote >}} Mt 6:21. {{< /sidenote >}} The Franciscan question is simple: Where is my treasure today? If we consent to the Spirit’s work, he will steadily relocate it—toward the Kingdom that does not wear out and toward the people for whom the King became poor.{{< sidenote >}} cf. Lk 12:33; 2 Cor 8:9. {{< /sidenote >}}
